The idea behind the planner

Motion first.
Imagine the world around it.

Learning from Hallucination (LfH) reverses the usual data-collection problem: take a feasible motion, then construct compatible obstacle scenes around it. This humanoid research applies that idea to the space swept by the whole body.

Explore how a motion becomes a training example, then a plan.

Training imagination. Runtime observation.

Here, the imagined scenes provide training context. At runtime the humanoid planner uses the supplied map, goal and motion history. The current research pipeline checks predicted execution and may refine the upper-body plan before sending a reference to SONIC. The public Navigate replays above use their explicitly identified frozen student bundle.

Where the idea comes from

LfH was introduced for robot navigation by Xiao, Liu, Warnell and Stone. The original method also hallucinates perception at deployment; the later HLSD work removes that step. Those papers establish the navigation idea; the whole-body adaptation shown here is this project's humanoid research.

    How it works

    Studio watches. It never drives.

    On the robot day everything runs on the deploy PC next to the G1. Studio attaches on loopback: it listens to the streams, and it may send node.py a checked goal or a request to stand. Click a part to see what it does.

    Scroll the diagram sideways to see all of it.

    Studio may send

    • goal to node.py, only after the checks pass and only while the robot stands still
    • stand to node.py
    • status requests, to read the goal back

    Studio never sends

    • go: the operator presses g on the node console
    • quit, or anything to the SONIC runner
    • any DDS message: it has no DDS participant and no motor-command path

    People keep

    • the wireless remote: D-up hands control to SONIC standing (not near overhead obstacles); Select is the runner's damping E-stop (the robot drops: gantry or spotter only)
    • the physical E-stop and a gantry or spotter
    • the decision to walk

    Exposure today: Studio binds only loopback, but node.py (tcp://*:5562, which accepts go and quit, and *:5556), the SONIC runner (*:5557) and the LIO bridge (*:5561) bind every interface. Proposal U3 to the deploy repository would bind node.py's two to loopback; the runner and the bridge need the same. Until then, firewall the robot network.

    Questions

    Fair questions.

    Is this the real policy?

    Yes: the deploy stack's own planner and tracker, recorded in simulation. The walks were made by the deploy stack's software: the frozen Flow student planner and the SONIC tracking policy, with Studio's reproduction of node.py's state machine, in MuJoCo with a simulated MID-360 LiDAR and the robot's deploy settings5. Your browser replays those recordings. Nothing here ran on a real robot.

    Why recorded, not live?

    So that no weights leave the research machine. A live policy in the browser would publish the SONIC release weights (NVIDIA Open Model License; the deploy repository deliberately does not redistribute them) and an export of the unpublished student planner, and download about 100 MB. Recording every reachable goal once keeps the weights on the research machine and makes the page work on a phone. Studio itself runs the stack live, locally.

    What does "arrived" mean?

    Arrived by the robot's own estimate. node.py's rule: its own pose estimate is within 0.4 m of the goal1. That is not the whole story, so every walk is also graded by contact2 and by the final distance by simulator truth3.

    Does it run on the real robot?

    Not yet on hardware. Studio's Deploy workspace relays the operator's presses (Arm, Start SONIC, Clear map, Send goal, GO) to the deploy kit's console, which owns the robot and every gate; it has run end to end in Practice on the real deploy chain in simulation, not on the robot. Navigate's robot link is tested against a protocol fixture. Readiness checklists and parsed code are not hardware verification.

    What counts as contact?

    More than 100 N against an obstacle, at any moment. The simulator's contact force between the robot and an obstacle. A walk is flagged when it exceeds 100 N at any frame2. The node's outcome does not see contact, so most such walks still "arrive".

    What is the plan body?

    The pose the tracker is chasing. The reference pose SONIC is tracking at each instant: 29 joint angles and an orientation, from the plan. Its pelvis position is the planner's and SONIC never receives it, so the body can fall behind or beside it4. Turn it on in the demo with B.

    Can I send it anywhere?

    Almost: to any recorded goal. Goals snap to recorded ones on a 0.4 m grid, every walk starts from the start mark, and each goal was recorded once with one seed. Spots that fail Studio's checks show why; that is what Studio would refuse to send.

    What does this page cost to load?

    Posters first. 3D and video on demand. Static files from GitHub Pages; nothing runs on a server. The page starts with text, images and compact recording summaries. Starting the demo loads about 1 MB for three.js, the robot and a room, then about 60 kB per walk. Highlight films download only when played and pause when hidden or scrolled away.

    1. Arrived. node.py's arrival rule: its own (LIO-level) pose estimate within the 0.4 m goal radius. The recording then ends when the robot stands still, or if it falls.
    2. Contact. Peak obstacle contact force over 100 N at any frame of the walk, simulator truth.
    3. Off by truth. Arrived by its estimate, but more than 0.6 m from the goal by simulator truth at the end. Moved away when its own estimate also ends more than 0.6 m away, as after a deferred stand that drifts.
    4. Plan to body. The distance between the plan body's pelvis (the reference root, the planner's) and the robot's own estimate of its pelvis, both in the planner's frame, over walking frames at 25 Hz.
    5. Simulation. CPU MuJoCo, planner budget 16 candidates and 10 ODE steps, 160 ms planner latency with predictive anchoring, LIO-level odometry drift, one memory per session, the current G1 actuator limits, seed 0.

    Use it

    Run it next to the robot.

    The page above is a demo of one workspace. This is the tool: it runs on loopback on the deploy PC.

    Run it

    git clone https://github.com/linjiw/g1-deploy-studio
cd g1-deploy-studio
python3 -m venv .venv
.venv/bin/pip install -r requirements.txt
npm ci
.venv/bin/python -m studio.server --port 8790
# then open http://127.0.0.1:8790/

    What you get from this repository alone: the gateway, the browser app and the tests; Replay draws the robot only when a recording points at a local G1 model. Rehearsals, Navigate's simulated robot and the robot link read the deploy stack (g1-student-deploy with the Flow student bundle and the SONIC policy), which is not public. No recordings, weights or robot meshes ship with the code.
